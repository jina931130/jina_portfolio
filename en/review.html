Chapter 01

Background and problem

01 · Background

Plenty of reviews, so why are they
hard to trust?

As AI-generated and manipulated reviews spread, shoppers are left to judge for themselves whether a review is genuine. Platforms have added verification badges and checking systems, yet review screens are still built around blocks of text. To find what they need for that judgement, people have to read long passages, and the mental effort adds up.

02 · Key Question

What if the trust cues were structured
on a single screen?

Review interfaces have evolved by adding individual trust cues such as verification badges and star ratings. How those cues should be structured on a single screen so that trust actually forms has not been tested enough. We therefore focused on the trust experience that people build while interacting with the interface.

Chapter 02

Design

03 · Trust Cues

We identified four key trust cues

We drew trust factors from earlier studies and removed anything that could not be manipulated visually in the experiment. Fifty-two people who had shopped online in the past six months then rated the importance of 18 items, and after dropping one top-rated item that was hard to visualise, we settled on four key attributes.

Total number of reviews

Decision

Items were dropped when they could not be changed visually on screen, however important they were. Testing the effect of a visualisation only works with attributes the screen can actually vary.

04 · Visualization Strategy

We designed three visualisation strategies

Each of the four attributes was visualised in three ways, each grounded in a different theory: one that speeds up judgement through information hierarchy, one that highlights social trust cues, and one that gives users more control and transparency.

V1

Information-efficient

Cognitive Load Theory (Sweller, 2011)

Minimal supporting information

Efficient visuals with little decoration

Related information grouped together

A clear hierarchy between items

V2

Social cue

Research on social trust cues (Choi & Leon, 2025)

Account credibility cues such as verification badges brought forward

Similarity cues placed in the reviewer profile

A summary of reviews by shoppers like me at the top

Social proof made as visible as possible

V3

Control and transparency

Self-determination theory · explainable system (XAI) principles

Filters that let users set the similarity conditions themselves

Reviews grouped by how similar the reviewer is

Accordions for deeper exploration

Stronger user control and information transparency

Decision

The four attributes stayed the same in every condition and only the visualisation changed, so any difference in the results can be read as an effect of the visualisation.

07 · Final Prototype

the final prototypes

The social cue (V2) and control and transparency (V3) versions kept their tested structures, with the similarity criteria adapted to the products used in the main study. 08 · Data Collection

We collected quantitative and qualitative data together

Thirteen participants (ten in their 20s, three in their 30s) went through all six conditions in a repeated-measures study. In each condition we observed how they explored the screen with think-aloud, then ran a 7-point Likert survey and a snapshot interview. After all six, a final interview drew out the differences between conditions and what drove their preferences.

13 · Insights

Trust in reviews came less from what was shown than from how it was shown

RQ1

How does visualising online reviews affect users?

Integrated visualisation directly shapes the trust experience

Simply structuring the reviews differently changed perceived usefulness, trust and purchase intention.

RQ2

Which design qualities make people experience review information as trustworthy?

Social context cues are the strongest trust factor

The social cue version gave concrete similarity cues such as household size and portion size, and put the most similar reviews first, cutting the effort of searching.

RQ3

How does the effect of visualised review information on trust judgement differ by product attribute type?

The right level of user control differs by product type

How much control to give should follow the cost of a wrong choice and how motivated people are to search.

15 · Limitations

Limitations and next steps

Limitation

Next step

Sample

Thirteen participants in their 20s and 30s were recruited by convenience, so the results cannot be generalised to wider groups. Comparisons were made on means without significance testing, so the size of the differences should be read with care.

Check whether the same pattern holds with a wider age range and a larger sample

Product range

Only two products were covered — a search good (laptop) and an experience good (meal kit) — so we could not confirm whether the pattern holds elsewhere.

Extend the test to categories with high uncertainty, such as health supplements and financial products

Timing of measurement

Only immediate reactions in a single session were measured, so how trust builds through repeated use remains unknown.

Use a longitudinal design with repeated visits to follow how trust builds over time
