Chapter 01

Background and questions

01 · Background

Drivers decide they must stop —
so why does the brake not follow?

Pedal misapplication happens most easily when drivers have to process several things at once: standing still, driving slowly, pulling away again. Even after a driver has seen the danger and decided what to do, that decision does not always turn into action.

02 · Core Concept

We defined the delay between
deciding and acting
as the judgment-action gap

We defined the judgment-action gap as the delay in which a driver has recognised the danger and decided to act, yet a shortage of cognitive resources keeps that decision from reaching the pedal.

03 · Research Question

How do a prompt's modality and timing
change behaviour correction?

Current in-car systems focus on limiting power or braking after a mistake has been made, and earlier studies have treated modality and timing separately. This study examined both together in a prompt that helps drivers correct their own behaviour.

RQ1 · Reaction time

How do modality and intervention timing affect the reaction time of behaviour correction?

RQ2 · Cognitive load

How does a driver's cognitive load change with modality and intervention timing?

RQ3 · Acceptance

How do drivers receive the conditions under which a prompt intervenes?

08 · Modality

The gaps between visual, auditory
and audiovisual were small

The differences in reaction time between visual, auditory and audiovisual prompts were not meaningful. At low speed the visual prompt in the centre of the cluster was no worse than the others, which differs from earlier work suggesting visual warnings are a disadvantage.

13 · Design Guideline

We sorted the nine combinations
into five levels of design priority

We placed reaction speed and cognitive load above user preference, and judged modality and timing as combinations rather than separately. Audiovisual at the optimal point met all three criteria: fast response, low cognitive load and driver acceptance.

14 · Final Prompt

We redesigned the prompt around the cluster edges and a warning tone

The visual prompt uses a red gradient along the edges of the cluster to reach peripheral vision, keeping the central panel dark so the instruction stands apart. The auditory prompt draws attention with a 988 Hz tone and then gives the instruction through a TTS voice saying “brake”.

Design points

Visual prompt

Background

We widened the stimulus to the whole cluster. A red gradient at 70–80% saturation along the edges reaches peripheral vision, while the centre stays dark so text and icons remain legible.

Presentation

No staged amber warning: red appears straight away at the optimal point.

Auditory prompt

Pairing

A tone (988 Hz · about 360 ms) catches attention, then a TTS voice says “brake” to give the action.

Volume

Drivers set the volume themselves, since hearing and driving conditions differ.

The tone and TTS work at the same optimal point as the visual stimulus. This design was derived from the experimental results and guidelines; its effect has not been tested separately.

16 · Contribution

We set criteria for designing timing
and modality as a combination

What corrects behaviour was less about which channel does the telling than about when the intervention comes, and modality worked through its combination with timing. Audiovisual at the optimal point is the benchmark that satisfies both reaction speed and cognitive load.

What this study contributes

Academic

Showed experimentally that the two variables must be treated together

By showing that modality and timing change responses through their combination, this complements driver-assistance research that has treated them separately. The interaction appeared only in reaction speed, which also shows that behavioural efficiency and cognitive cost must be read together.

Based on · 09 Timing · 10 Interaction

Methodological

Showed that the conditions people find comfortable may not be the ones that perform

Analysing the experiment alongside the interviews showed that auditory-only and early timing, both rated favourably, were in fact the slowest. Designing on perception alone can cost performance.

Based on · 12 Preference vs Performance

Practical

Carried the design guidelines through to an actual cluster design

We derived guidelines for channel, intervention timing and combination design, and built the audiovisual × optimal combination into a cluster design.

Based on · 13 Design Guideline · 14 Final Prompt
